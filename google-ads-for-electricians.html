Google Ads for Electricians

If you’re an electrician looking to book more jobs, Google Ads can get you in front of people already searching for help. But the setup matters. I see businesses put every service into one ad group, send everyone to the homepage, and then wonder why the leads aren’t coming in.

My approach is straightforward: match the search to the service, make it easy to get in touch, and track what happens after the click. Let’s work through it.

By Alex · Updated October 28, 2026 · 10 min read

Why I Use Google Ads for Electrical Businesses

Think about what someone types when they need an electrician: “electrician near me,” “emergency electrician,” or something specific like “light fixture installation.” Those searches tell you a lot about the work they need.

In a large market like Toronto, you can expect competition. That makes the ad, the landing page, and the way you group services worth paying attention to. In the tutorial, light-fixture installation is my example: it deserves its own message instead of another generic electrician ad.

If you’re deciding how paid search fits with other channels, my electrician lead generation guide covers the wider plan.

01

Set Up Call and Form Tracking Before Spending

I say this a few times in the tutorial because it’s easy to overlook: track your phone calls and form submissions before you spend money. Otherwise, you’re paying for traffic without a clear way to see whether it produces inquiries.

I want to see calls from the ad, calls from the landing page, and successful form submissions. A click on a phone number is useful information, but it doesn’t tell you whether anyone answered or whether the caller needed a service you offer.

Use the Google tag or Google Tag Manager to set up the actions you need, and check your Google Ads and GA4 reporting where you use both. Avoid counting the same lead twice as a primary conversion. Then record the next steps: relevant inquiry, estimate, booked job, and completed work.

02

Start With a Focused Search Campaign

For the setup I show, I choose a Search campaign aimed at generating leads. I name it clearly so I can identify the service and market later. Something like “Electrician — Toronto — Search” is enough.

In this tutorial, I opt out of the Display Network and Search partners at launch. That gives me a narrower starting point to review. Other networks can be tested separately once I know what the core Search traffic is doing.

I don’t spend the launch adding lots of audience segments. I start with the customer’s search and the service area, then assess any audience test on its results. If you add an audience, check whether it’s set to observation or targeting so you understand how it affects reach.

03

Check Locations and Schedule Ads Around Real Coverage

Open the location options and look at what you’re including. For an electrician serving a local area, I usually start with Presence: people in or regularly in your included locations. The broader presence-or-interest option can reach people interested in the area who aren’t there.

Then open the map. Check the cities, boundaries, and radiuses instead of assuming the selection matches your coverage. Exclude areas you can’t serve, and consider travel time as well as distance. Google’s location signals aren’t exact, so check the addresses on actual inquiries too. Google explains the location options here.

Set an ad schedule that fits your team. People look for electrical help after work and on weekends, so those hours may be worth testing if you can answer. Emergency ads need emergency coverage. If you only handle planned work, make the response time clear.

04

Build Ad Groups Around the Electrical Services You Want

I normally start with the core electrician service, then add specific services as the budget allows. In the tutorial, I also like to give “near me” searches their own ad group so I can write and review that message separately. It’s an approach to test; you don’t need a new group for every slight variation.

Light-fixture installation is a good example of a focused service group. Panel upgrades, EV chargers, generators, rewiring, and emergency repairs can follow the same pattern when those are jobs you want.

Find Electrician Keywords and Add Negatives

In Keyword Planner, set your actual area before reviewing terms. Start with “electrician,” then work through the services and local variations. Check that the keyword describes work you offer; search volume alone isn’t a reason to add it.

I use exact and phrase match in the tutorial. Exact match uses square brackets, such as [electrician Toronto]; phrase match uses quotation marks, such as “light fixture installation”. Both can match by meaning, so you still need to review search terms. Google’s match-type guide explains how reach differs.

Negative keyword themes I would review

  • Employment: jobs, salary, apprenticeships, and careers.
  • Education: courses, training, exams, and certification classes.
  • DIY and research: wiring diagrams, tutorials, and how-to instructions.
  • Products: wholesale supplies, parts, tools, and fixtures for sale.
  • Wrong fit: locations or services your business does not cover.

Check the full search before blocking it. For example, someone looking for a certified electrician may be a good lead, while someone looking for a certification course isn’t. I also avoid expanding into broad match casually; it needs a suitable bidding approach and reliable conversion data.

Send Each Ad to a Landing Page That Fits the Job

This is another point I keep coming back to: use a focused landing page. If I’m advertising light-fixture installation, I want the person to land on a page about light-fixture installation, with an obvious way to call or request an estimate.

The page can be part of your website. What matters is how well it answers that search and helps someone take the next step. A generic contact page usually gives them too little information to make a decision.

  • Name the service and the areas you cover near the top.
  • Show your real licensing, insurance, reviews, and relevant project work.
  • Explain the process, availability, service-call fee, or pricing factors.
  • Make the phone number easy to use on mobile.
  • Keep the form short: service needed, location, contact details, and timing.
  • Explain what happens after the customer gets in touch.

For a panel or charger project, you may need more details about the property, equipment, permits, and estimate process. Build the page around what that customer needs to know.

Write Ads With the Service, Location, and a Clear Next Step

My starting point for headlines is simple: the service, the location, a useful reason to call, and the action. In the Toronto example, that might mean “Toronto Electrician,” “Book a Service Call,” or “Light Fixture Installation.” Use your own service area.

If a callout fee helps qualify people, make it clear. If you offer same-day appointments or emergency service, only say so when you can provide them. I want the ad to set the right expectation before someone clicks.

Example ad direction: “Light Fixture Installation” · “Serving Toronto” · “Book a Service Call.” A description could read: “Need new lighting installed? Tell us about your fixtures and location. Call to discuss your project.”

Fill out the available headline and description options with useful variations. Review the combinations and test changes. I don’t pin every headline by default; I reserve pinning for messages that need to stay in a specific place. If eligible, add your verified business name and logo too.

Keep Call Assets, Sitelinks, and Callouts Relevant

Add a call asset so customers can reach you from the ad, then make sure the number and call tracking work. Use sitelinks for useful destinations, such as the advertised service, project examples, or booking information.

In the tutorial, I prefer service-specific assets at the ad-group level. That helps me keep an emergency message with emergency ads and an installation message with installation ads. Shared assets are fine when they apply everywhere; check what each ad group can inherit.

Useful callouts might explain coverage, a real service-call fee, or availability. Keep each claim accurate and review assets alongside the calls and bookings they help produce.

Work Out a Google Ads Budget for Your Market

In my Toronto example, I discuss click estimates around $5–$15 and use roughly ten clicks a day as an initial planning check. At $15 a click, that works out to about $150 a day. Those are examples from the tutorial, so pull fresh estimates for your own market.

I also discuss $50 a day as a practical lower starting point for that type of setup. I wouldn’t treat it as a rule for every electrician. Your services, competition, landing page, and margins all affect what makes a useful test.

If you have $30 a day and clicks are costing $15, you’re buying about two clicks a day. That can make learning slow. I would narrow the service and area first. My Facebook Ads guide for electricians covers another option for planned projects and offers.

Keep three costs separate: a click, a lead, and a booked job. As an illustration, a $10 click with a 10% click-to-lead rate produces a $100 lead cost. If one in four leads books, the ad cost is $400 per booked job. Those assumptions are examples; replace them with your own numbers.

Repeat work, referrals, and reviews can add value over time. I still want to see the first-job economics clearly, then measure repeat value as it happens.

Choose Bidding You Can Monitor

In this tutorial, I start with Manual CPC because I want control over bids while I check the searches coming in. I use Keyword Planner estimates as a starting point, then adjust based on traffic and actual results. A first-page bid estimate is useful context, not a target you have to chase.

If you use Maximize Clicks for a Search campaign, set a sensible CPC limit and review it. That strategy aims to get clicks within the budget; it doesn’t by itself establish whether those clicks become useful leads. Google documents Manual CPC and Maximize Clicks in more detail.

As the account develops, I reassess the bidding against reliable conversion data and booked work. Track the outcomes first so any later automation has a useful goal.

Review Lead Quality and Follow Up Quickly

Listen to what people actually need, where they’re located, and whether you can help. Record service type, property details, urgency, estimate status, and the reason an inquiry did or didn’t become work. This is where you find out whether a campaign that looks good in Google Ads is good for the business.

Answer calls while ads are running, confirm form inquiries, and explain the fee and next available appointment. If calls are being missed, fix that before increasing the budget. Before you publish

The Mistakes I Would Check First

  • Call and form tracking hasn’t been tested.
  • Every electrical service shares one vague ad and page.
  • The location settings reach areas the team can’t cover.
  • The ad promises availability or pricing the business can’t deliver.
  • Assets from another service show up in the wrong ad group.
  • Clicks are being judged without reviewing calls and job outcomes.

After launch

How I Would Use the First 90 Days

Month 1: check the setup

Test conversions, launch the priority services, review search terms, and confirm calls reach someone who can book the work.

Month 2: improve the leads

Review which services and areas produce useful inquiries. Improve ads and pages, add negatives carefully, and check follow-up.

Month 3: use job results

Compare booked work and acquisition cost. Test bidding or expand a service when the results and capacity support it.

You may see traffic soon after approval, but lead timing varies. I would agree on a budget and review period up front, then use the data to decide what comes next.

Google Ads for Electricians FAQ

Do Google Ads work for electricians?

They can put you in front of people actively searching for electrical work. I focus on the service they need, the area you cover, a relevant landing page, and tracking that shows which inquiries become jobs.

How much should an electrician spend on Google Ads?

In my Toronto tutorial, I discuss $50 to $150 per day as planning examples. Your starting budget should come from local click estimates, service margins, and a test you can afford. Those tutorial figures are not a universal minimum or a current price quote.

What electrician keywords should I start with?

Start with your core services: electrician, local electrician, residential electrician, electrician near me, and your city. Build separate themes for services such as emergency electrical repairs, light-fixture installation, panel upgrades, or EV charger installation when you offer them.

Should I use Manual CPC or Maximize Clicks?

In the tutorial, I start with Manual CPC so I can control bids while reviewing the traffic. If I use Maximize Clicks for a Search campaign, I set a sensible CPC limit. I reassess bidding against reliable conversion and job data as the account develops.

Do I need a landing page for electrician ads?

I recommend a focused page for each main service you advertise. The page should explain the service, coverage, proof, fees or pricing factors, and how to call or request an estimate.

Should I advertise in the evenings and on weekends?

There may be useful demand outside normal working hours. I would test those hours if you can answer calls and fulfill the work. Only advertise emergency or round-the-clock availability when you can deliver it.

How soon should I expect leads?

An approved campaign may begin receiving traffic soon after launch, but the timing and number of inquiries vary. I check tracking and search quality first, then evaluate qualified inquiries and booked jobs over the agreed test period.

What should I track before launching?

Track calls from ads, calls from the landing page, and successful form submissions. Then record whether each inquiry was relevant, became an estimate, and turned into a booked or completed job.
